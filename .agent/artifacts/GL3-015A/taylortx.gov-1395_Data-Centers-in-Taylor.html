Data Centers in Taylor

Recent Activities Surrounding Data Centers

Petition

(June 30, 2026) At the June 11 City Council Meeting, a coalition of residents presented the City with a petition calling for a temporary ban on the development of data centers in Taylor until a dedicated zone for digital infrastructure is created to better regulate and manage their use. The City is working to validate the signatures and seeking legal council on the next steps. We appreciate the tremendous effort that residents have engaged in to express their concerns about data centers. 

Land Development Code Priority Revisions

(June 30, 2026) At the May 6, 2026 Special Called City Council Workshop, the City Council directed staff and our consultant to develop priority amendments to the City's Comprehensive Plan and Land Development Code to address ongoing development challenges including data center regulation. Staff are working with Freese and Nichols LLC. to develop an ordinance that regulates intensive uses like data centers. These regulations will address water use, noise, light, air pollution, and buffers with residential areas. Once the draft is complete, it will be presented for community input before it proceeds through the process for adoption.

Questions we are hearing:

What are data centers?

Data centers are large facilities that house the computer servers and networking equipment that power the internet — cloud storage, streaming services, financial transactions, healthcare records, and AI applications all run on hardware housed in facilities like these. They are capital-intensive, representing hundreds of millions to billions of dollars in taxable property value, but they employ relatively few permanent workers compared to manufacturing plants.

Why are data center developers interested in Taylor?

Data center projects are under construction across the country due to rising demand for data-intensive services requiring high-powered computing. Many project developers are interested in locating in Central Texas because of the region’s available land, expanding power infrastructure, and a favorable tax environment. The Samsung project — and infrastructure investments made to accommodate Samsung — has made this area even more attractive to some data center developers.

How do data centers benefit the Taylor community?

Typically featuring hundreds of millions of dollars in equipment, data centers carry high appraised values and generate significant long-term tax revenue for the City and school district. Because data centers don’t employ many people or attract many visitors, they generate little traffic or emergency service calls. They don’t place extra demand on housing, classrooms, and public services. 

Do data centers create jobs?

Permanent employment at a data center is limited — typically a small operations and security staff. The value these facilities provide is property tax revenue, not a large local workforce. A billion-dollar facility with a small permanent staff still contributes substantial tax revenue to city services and the school district year after year, with minimal cost to serve.

Will data centers strain Taylor's water supply?

Data centers that use city water must operate closed-loop cooling systems that recycle water internally rather than continuously drawing fresh supply. A closed-loop data center's ongoing water use is comparable to that of about 15 single-family homes, not the millions of gallons per day that older evaporative cooling systems consume. Our current infrastructure could not support a data center without a closed-loop system.

Will data centers affect my electricity service or bill?

The City of Taylor does not have a municipal electric utility. Taylor residents and businesses must get power from Oncor, a private electric utility serving the Taylor area. Oncor — along with ERCOT, the state grid operator — determines whether sufficient power capacity exists to serve a new development. 

A 2025 state law (Senate Bill 6) requires any facility requesting 75 or more megawatts of power to pay for an initial transmission study, demonstrate site control, and fund the cost of any transmission infrastructure upgrades needed to connect to the grid. Facilities that interconnect after December 31, 2025 must also be capable of curtailing their load or deploying backup generation when ERCOT declares a grid emergency. 

Texas is experiencing a high demand for data centers. Because we are on a state-wide electric grid and do not have our own system, the supply of electricity and demand for electricity in any part of the State may affect Taylor. In other words, data centers will use Texas electricity whether they are in Taylor or somewhere else in the State. Having them locate closer to the City may provide some benefits to the reliability of our local electric services as they get upgraded to support these larger demands.

Does the City provide financial incentives for data centers?

We consider every economic development project that requests incentives on a case-by-case basis with capital investment and job creation serving as two primary considerations we use to determine the long-term benefits to the community. While data centers do not typically employ many people, they can provide big economic benefits due to the hundreds of millions of dollars in capital investment required and significant tax revenue generated. To date, Taylor has provided economic incentives to one proposed data center. Click here to learn about the Blueprint Projects Data Center.

Can the City just say no to data centers?

In short, no. Cities in Texas cannot simply exclude data centers from the city, especially if we allow much more intrusive and heavier uses in the city such as industrial manufacturing. Much of Taylor's available industrial and employment center land was zoned for industrial uses years ago, through comprehensive planning processes that involved public input. When land is already zoned for industrial use and a project meets all applicable requirements, Texas law gives that project a legal right to proceed. Our job is to require the project to meet Taylor's design, water, noise, and setback standards — then ensure those standards are met. 

The City is in the process of developing strict standards for data center development that will clearly define protections for the general public and residential areas including noise, light, and setbacks. In the mean-time, the City can and has negotiated with developers to ensure these standards are met and residents are protected. 

What is the public process for approving these projects?

The process of approval for a data center is different depending on the type of zoning that the property has at the time of development. In Taylor, data centers can only be built in areas that allow industrial uses such as the Employment Center zone. If a developer would like to build on a property with different zoning, they would first have to hold public hearings at the Planning and Zoning Commission and the City Council. Those hearings include a public notice mailed to adjacent property owners, signage at the property, and a notice on our website. The City Council will then receive the input from Planning and Zoning as well as the public before considering the zone change. If, however, the property is already zoned Employment Center, the owners have the right to build a data center. Per state law, the City cannot place undue burden that may delay or prevent the construction of the data center in that case. The City's only role is to ensure the development meets our building codes. 

Do data centers affect property values nearby?

Available evidence from peer cities such as Round Rock does not show that data centers decrease nearby residential property values. Data centers produce no traffic congestion, have minimal permanent staffing, and are quieter than the manufacturing or warehousing operations that could often be built on the same industrial land without additional City Council approval.

That said, any large industrial facility near a neighborhood can present quality-of-life concerns that the City requires developers to address through buffers, screening, landscaping, setbacks, noise, and other measures.

What does the City do to mitigate noise from data centers?

Data centers generally produce less loud noise than many other large manufacturing facilities. Large cooling systems produce a low-frequency sound that Taylor requires project owners to mitigate by providing a setback/buffer between data center facilities and residential properties. We continue to work within the limits of state law and developer negotiations to strengthen those standards where we can.
